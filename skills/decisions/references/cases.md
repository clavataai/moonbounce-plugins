# Writing cases

How the build and improve flows write their cases: fresh case writers, run at once, each labeling its own cases, merged into one file and checked with `jq`. Your flow's guide says where the cases go and how many to write. The commands here name the build flow's dataset, `moonbounce/<decision_id>.examples.jsonl`; the improve flow writes its synthetic set, `moonbounce/<decision_id>.synthetic.jsonl`, in its place. The line format is in [A decision's files](files.md).

## Hard cases first

The cases are there to find what the brief and the decision get wrong, so most of them should be hard. Two agents reading the same brief agree on easy cases and learn nothing from them; the cases that teach are the ones a careful reader of the brief could call either way. The case writer and the build flow's blind writer look for:

- one fact either side of a line, where it alone decides the outcome (the order at $250 and at $251, day 30 and day 31);
- two clauses pulling opposite ways on the same case;
- what the policy doesn't mention;
- wording that hedges or misleads: keywords that point one way while the facts point the other;
- the message and the order disagreeing;
- phrasing and form: indirect, multilingual, long or short, irrelevant detail, fields the brief says can be missing.

Beside them, a thin floor of plain cases, one per clause, so the decision is checked on the obvious calls too. Every outcome appears, `abstain` and `escalate` included. Each case names in its reason the clause and the line it tests.

Size the set from the brief, and cap it at **about five cases a clause**: for each clause, a boundary pair for each line it draws (two cases, one fact apart), one the policy doesn't cover or whose wording misleads, and one plain case; and one case for each pair of clauses likely to collide. A brief with seven one-line clauses and seven collisions comes to 35 cases. More add little the red team and the blind check won't find, and every refine round scores them all.

## The person's data first

When the person brought labeled data, put it into the dataset yourself before any writer starts, so no writer retypes it: one line each, `{item_id, state, attribute_values?, metadata: {origin: "user"}, claims: [{kind: "expected_outcome", value}], label_source, reason}`, with their item ids, labels, `label_source` and reasons as they are and `state` as the brief's Inputs say the app sends it (a ticket's `text` may be the app's `state.message`, for instance). When their fields map straight onto the Inputs, that is one `jq` over their file, written to scratch and merged into `<examples>` as the writers' files are below. When the mapping needs reading, give it to a fresh subagent on a smaller model, writing to scratch. Either way, before merging, check a sample of lines against their file, labels and reasons included, and check that no item id repeats (the check below): take repeated ids in their own data to the person. The answered probes already there (the build flow's) stay as they are.

Those lines go in with their label, `label_source` and `reason` as they are. Use `label_source: "human"` only when the person says they or their team labeled it, and `unknown` when nobody knows who did. No case writer relabels them, or the labeled probes.

## Case writers

**Split the clauses.** A writer produces its cases one after another, so one writer for the whole set is slow. Run several at once, each owning a share of the brief's clauses: about three clauses (some 15 cases) a writer, up to four writers, and one writer when the brief has three clauses or fewer. A writer owns both sides of each of its lines, so a boundary pair never splits between two writers.

**Assign the collisions** before briefing anyone. For each clause, name the other clause it most likely collides with (two clauses pulling opposite ways on one case), and list the pairs, each once. Each pair goes to the writer of its lower-numbered clause, so no two writers write the same collision, and a writer may own several or none. Don't split by outcome: a writer given one outcome writes the cases it is sure of, and the two halves of a boundary pair would land with writers who never see each other's. Each writer writes to its own files, since parallel appends to one file can interleave.

Brief a fresh case writer for each share, all in one message so they run at once, numbering them `<w>` from 1: "Write cases for the decision in `<brief>`, for clauses `<clauses>` only, as `<cases guide>` § Hard cases first lists: for each clause, a boundary pair for each line it draws, one the policy doesn't cover or whose wording misleads, and one plain case; and one case for each of these collisions, which are yours alone: `<collisions>`. Write no other collision, and no more than `<most>` cases in all. Never write two cases that test the same line with the same deciding facts, and none that a line in `<examples>` already tests that way: where the person's data or a probe already covers one of your cases, leave it out. Mark your cases `metadata.origin: "synthetic"`, list the numbers of the clauses each tests in `metadata.clauses` as one string, ascending, with commas and no spaces (`"3,7"`), mark each collision case `metadata.collision` with its pair written the same way, with item ids `syn-<k>` numbered from `<first>` and group names starting `w<w>-`. Put a boundary pair (one fact apart, different outcomes) in two groups, and paraphrase twins with the same outcome in one. Use only the `state` keys, attribute values, types and ranges the brief lists. Write each case to `<cases>` as one example, labeled with the outcome the brief gives it, spelled exactly as the brief's Outcomes section names it: `{item_id, state, attribute_values?, metadata: {cell, clauses, group, origin}, claims: [{kind: "expected_outcome", value: <outcome>}], label_source: "synthetic", reason: <one line citing a clause>}`. The lines in `<examples>` whose `metadata.origin` is `user` or whose `label_source` is `human` carry the person's labels: treat them as precedent. Give a synthetic case close to one of their lines the same label, and where the brief would label such a case otherwise, write it with no `claims` or `label_source`, `metadata.conflicts_with` set to their line's item id and a `reason` naming the conflict, instead of choosing. Read nothing else. Work only inside the repo. Write files with your file tool, never with a heredoc or `echo`; a `cp` or a `jq` to a file under `moonbounce/` is fine, but never onto a file the command reads. Run one plain command at a time: no `cd`, `;` or `&&` chains, loops, shell variables, `$(…)` or heredocs. Never put the person's data or item ids in a command." `<first>` is 100 × (`<w>` − 1) + 1 (above any `syn-` id already in the dataset), `<collisions>` the pairs assigned to the writer ("none" when it has none), `<most>` four a clause, two more for each line a clause draws beyond its first, and one a collision, and `<cases>` is `cases-<w>.jsonl` in `moonbounce/scratch/<decision_id>/` (create it first with `mkdir -p`).

## Merging

When every writer is done, merge their files in writer order, one command at a time. The merging `jq` writes a whole file to scratch and refuses a line that doesn't parse. It reads the dataset as well as the writers' files, so create it empty with the file tool when it doesn't exist yet. The check after it prints any item id that repeats. Copy the scratch file over the original with `cp` only when the `jq` printed no error and the check printed nothing; never redirect onto a file the command reads, since the shell empties it first. When the check prints ids, don't copy that file. If every one of the writers' lines is already in the original, unchanged, that file was merged before, so leave it and go on to the next. Otherwise ids collide, between two writers or with lines already there: have the writer whose ids repeat renumber its file into an unused hundred, then merge again. Writing whole files, never appending, also keeps a file without a final newline from running two lines together. Delete the writers' files only once they are merged, so a later merge takes only new cases:

```sh
jq -c . moonbounce/<decision_id>.examples.jsonl moonbounce/scratch/<decision_id>/cases-*.jsonl > moonbounce/scratch/<decision_id>/examples.jsonl
jq -cs 'group_by(.item_id)[] | select(length > 1) | .[0].item_id' moonbounce/scratch/<decision_id>/examples.jsonl
cp moonbounce/scratch/<decision_id>/examples.jsonl moonbounce/<decision_id>.examples.jsonl
rm moonbounce/scratch/<decision_id>/cases-*.jsonl
```

## Duplicates and outcomes

With each collision owned by one writer, writers can't overlap, so check that rather than search for near-duplicates: these checks find cases with the same content and collisions written twice, not paraphrases, and a paraphrase that slips through costs one extra line in each scoring. The first two commands print nothing when all is well: the first lists cases with the same content, the second the collisions that more than one writer tagged as theirs (a writer is known by its hundred of `syn-` ids; a tag in another form is left out, and the next command lists it). A writer's tag in the wrong form would hide a collision it wrote twice, so list those too: `jq -cs '[.[] | select((.item_id | startswith("syn-")) and (.metadata.collision != null) and ((.metadata.collision | tostring | test("^[0-9]+,[0-9]+$") and (split(",") | unique | length == 2)) | not)) | .item_id]' moonbounce/<decision_id>.examples.jsonl` prints `[]` when every tag is well formed; fix any it lists to the `"3,7"` form: two different clause numbers. An untagged collision only the first command can catch, when its content repeats. The third counts the cases per label: an outcome of the brief's that it leaves out has none.

```sh
jq -cs 'group_by([.state, (.attribute_values // {})])[] | select(length > 1) | map(.item_id)' moonbounce/<decision_id>.examples.jsonl
jq -cs '[.[] | select((.item_id | startswith("syn-")) and (.metadata.collision != null) and (.metadata.collision | tostring | test("^[0-9]+,[0-9]+$") and (split(",") | unique | length == 2)))] | group_by(.metadata.collision | tostring | split(",") | map(tonumber) | sort)[] | select((map((.item_id | ltrimstr("syn-") | tonumber? // 0) - 1 | . / 100 | floor) | unique | length) > 1) | map(.item_id)' moonbounce/<decision_id>.examples.jsonl
jq -cs '[.[].claims[]? | select(.kind == "expected_outcome") | .value] | group_by(.) | map({(.[0]): length}) | add' moonbounce/<decision_id>.examples.jsonl
```

Every label the count shows must be an outcome the brief's Outcomes section names: fix a misspelling, and take an outcome the brief doesn't name to the person. For an outcome with none, write one plain case yourself that gives it, citing its clause, in place of that clause's plain case (so the clause stays within its cap): give it the next unused id in that clause's writer's hundred, drop the writer's plain case for the clause (its `cell` says plain) as duplicates are dropped below, and count again.

For each group the first two commands print, read its cases. Of two that test the same line with the same deciding facts and the same label, drop the one from the higher-numbered writer: write the dropped ids as a JSON array to `moonbounce/scratch/<decision_id>/drop-ids.json` with the file tool, fresh for this drop, then run `jq -cs '.[0] as $ids | if ($ids | type) != "array" then error("the ids file is missing or not a JSON array") else .[1:][] | select(.item_id | IN($ids[]) | not) end' moonbounce/scratch/<decision_id>/drop-ids.json moonbounce/<decision_id>.examples.jsonl`, written to scratch and copied back as above. When their labels differ, keep both: the writers read the brief two ways, which is a conflict for the person (below). Otherwise a writer wrote a collision it doesn't own: keep the owning writer's case. On a return (below), a collision that an earlier writer and the new one both wrote is expected: read it for the same call written twice, as above, and nothing more.

## A return

After a brief change that added a clause or moved a line, the person's data and the earlier cases are already in. Brief writers only for the clauses that changed, each with the next unused hundred for its item ids and a new group prefix, and merge the same way.

## Conflicts go to the person

Before the decision is written or changed for them: each pair of duplicates whose writers labeled them differently, and each case a writer left unlabeled because the brief would label it against the person's own data (`metadata.conflicts_with`). Ask blind, one case a question, as step 4 of [Check before you fit](refine.md#check-before-you-fit) asks: the case alone, then which side said what. Write their call into the line (`claims`, `label_source: "human"`, their `reason`); when it says something the brief doesn't, change the brief, as your flow's guide says for a brief change. A conflict with their own data is either a gap in the brief or a label of theirs they now see differently: show them their reason beside the writer's, and let them say which.
